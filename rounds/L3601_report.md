## Background
- **Midy's theorem:** for a prime p whose repeating block has even length L, the two halves of the block add to 99…9, so the digit sum is 9L/2. Examples: 1/17 → 72, 1/89 → 198.
- **The real condition** is **10^(L/2) ≡ −1 (mod n)**, the "complement property." It can hold for composites too, e.g. 1/437 = 1/(19 × 23), digit sum 891.

## Reading
- **P4 gives an exact, checkable rule for when the halves complement.** For each prime-power factor, look at its period and count how many times 2 divides it. If that count is the same (and at least 1) for every factor, the property holds.
  - **437 = 19 × 23:** the periods are 18 = 2 × 9 and 22 = 2 × 11, one factor of 2 each, so the property holds.
  - **21 = 3 × 7:** the periods are 1 (no factor of 2) and 6 (one), so it fails.
  - **Other composites with the property below 1,000:** 49, 77, 91, 121, 133, 143, 161, 169, 209, 247, 253, 289, 299, …
  - This is standard number theory (−1 lies in the cyclic subgroup generated by 10 exactly under this condition). P4 confirms it computationally to 10,000.
- **P2 failed:** 9L/2 also appears *without* complementary halves in 29 cases below 1,000.
  - **The cases:** 21, 57, 147, 171, 183, 287, 291, 327, 357, 377, 403, 427, …
  - Many are multiples of 3.
  - **For these, the digit sum lands on 9L/2 by another route,** not by the half-complement mechanism. So **a digit sum of 9L/2 alone does not prove the halves complement.**
